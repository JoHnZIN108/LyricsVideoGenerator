The AI doesn't remember your conversation. At all.

The AI reads the whole thing, top to bottom, writes the next reply... and then forgets it all. Until you send another message, and it reads everything again from scratch.

[SLIDE 4: A whiteboard filling up with writing]

Now here's where the whiteboard comes in.

There's a limit to how much the AI can read at once. It's called the context window. Think of it as a whiteboard. Everything in your chat has to fit on it.

[SLIDE 8: "The fixes"]

Okay. The fixes.

New topic, new chat. If you've been planning a holiday for an hour and now want help with your CV, open a fresh chat. Clean whiteboard, better answers.

[SLIDE 9: "The summary trick"]

And my favorite. When a chat gets long and the answers start getting weird, ask it, "summarize everything important from this conversation." Copy that summary. Open a new chat. Paste it in.

You just wiped the whiteboard and rewrote only the stuff that matters. The answers get sharp again. Almost every time.

So next time the AI forgets something, don't get annoyed. It's not ignoring you. It's rereading everything, every time, on a whiteboard that's only so big.

Title: Why AI Gives a Different Answer Every Time Thumbnail text: IT ROLLS DICE Short to cut: Slides 3 and 4, the weighted dice (about 40 seconds) Length: about 690 words, around 5 minutes

[SLIDE 1: Same question, two different answers side by side]

[SLIDE 3: Weighted dice graphic: mat 60%, floor 20%, sofa 15%, other 5%]

Now it has to pick one. And this is the moment everything changes.

It doesn't always pick the top word. Instead, imagine a weighted dice. "Mat" takes up most of the sides. "Floor" gets a few. "Moon" gets a tiny sliver. Then it rolls.

[SLIDE 4: A sentence branching into different paths]

That's why you never get the exact same reply twice.

Okay, fair question. Why not just always pick the most likely word? Wouldn't that be more accurate?

Remember the phone autocomplete game from my ChatGPT video? You keep tapping the middle suggestion and get, "I'm going to be there in a few minutes and I will be there in a few minutes and..."

That's what always picking the top word does. It gets stuck in loops. It sounds flat and robotic. A little bit of randomness is what makes the writing sound natural. Almost human.

[SLIDE 6: A dial from "Predictable" to "Wild"]

And there's actually a dial for this. It's called temperature.

Turn it low, and the AI plays it safe. Picks the likely words, gives similar answers every time. Turn it up, and it takes more risks. More creative... and at the top of the dial, honestly, more unhinged.

As a software engineer, when I build things with AI, this is one of the first settings I think about. Pulling facts out of a document? Low. Brainstorming names for something? Higher.

That's closed AI. The company keeps the model on its own computers. You use it through their app or their service, but you never get the file. Most of the big-name flagship AI assistants work this way.

[SLIDE 3: "Open" with a recipe card being photocopied]

Now picture a chef who publishes their recipe online. Anyone can download it. Cook it at home. Tweak it, add more spice, open their own restaurant with it.

That's open AI models. The company releases the model file itself. Companies like Meta and Mistral, and Chinese labs like DeepSeek and Alibaba, have all released models that anyone can download.

[SLIDE 4: "Open-ish" footnote]

Quick nerdy footnote, because someone in the comments will say it. A lot of these are technically "open weights," not fully open source. You get the finished recipe, the numbers, but usually not all the ingredients they trained it on, or every step of how they made it. Some also come with rules about how you're allowed to use them.

So "open" in AI is more of a spectrum than a yes or no.

[SLIDE 5: "The case for open"]

Okay, the fight. Here's the case people make for open.

Anyone can look inside and study it, so researchers can check for problems instead of trusting a company's word. It's cheaper, because you're not paying someone every time you use it. You can run it on your own computer, so your data never leaves your house. And it stops a handful of big companies from controlling the most important technology of our time.

[SLIDE 6: "The case for closed"]

And the case people make for keeping it closed.

Once you release a model, you can't take it back. Ever. If someone finds a way to misuse it, it's already out there on thousands of computers. The safety features built into a model can also be stripped out by whoever downloads it. And as AI gets more powerful, some people worry that giving everyone the full recipe for the most capable systems is a risk we can't undo.

There are business reasons too, obviously. A secret recipe is easier to charge for.

[SLIDE 7: A scale, balanced]

So who's right? Honestly, both sides have real points. And a lot of people land somewhere in the middle. Open is great for smaller models. Be more careful with the most powerful ones.

I'm not going to tell you what to think here. But now you can follow the argument when you hear it.

[SLIDE 8: "Why this matters to you"]

So why should you care, if you just use AI on your phone?

First, privacy. You can run a small open model on a decent laptop with no internet at all. Nothing you type goes anywhere. For really sensitive stuff, that's a big deal.

Second, a lot of the AI apps you use from smaller companies are built on open models underneath. It's why there are suddenly so many AI apps. Someone took a free recipe and opened a restaurant.

And third, price. Free recipes floating around put pressure on everyone to make AI cheaper.

[SLIDE 9: Safe vs recipe book, recap]

So. Closed AI is the secret sauce you can taste but never see. Open AI is the recipe anyone can download and cook. Both are shaping what AI you get to use, and what it costs.

[SLIDE 5: Word 4: "AGENT" slapped as a sticker on lots of products]

Here's the thing most people don't know. There's no agreed definition. None.

Script 19: AI jargon speed round

Title: 10 AI Terms in 5 Minutes (Speed Round) Thumbnail text: 10 TERMS. 5 MINUTES. Short to cut: Any single term. Each one works as its own 30-second Short, so this video gives you up to 10 Shorts Length: about 720 words, around 5 minutes

[SLIDE 1: "10 TERMS / 5 MINUTES" with a countdown timer graphic]

Ten AI terms. Five minutes. About thirty seconds each. By the end of this, you'll be able to read an AI headline without Googling half the words.

There's a timer on screen. Let's see if I can beat it. And stay for number ten, because it's the one that helps you see through the charts in every AI launch.

[SLIDE 2: Term 1: "LLM" with timer running]

Number one. LLM. Large language model.

It's the proper name for the thing behind ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini. "Large" because it's huge, billions of knobs. "Language" because it works with text. "Model" because, remember, it's the learned recipe.

When people say "an LLM," they basically mean the chatty kind of AI.

[SLIDE 3: Term 2: "TOKEN"]

Number two. Token.

The chunks AI reads and writes in. Sometimes a whole word, sometimes a piece of one. Roughly, a token is about three quarters of a word in English.

You'll see it in pricing. AI companies often charge per token. More text, more tokens, more cost.

[SLIDE 4: Term 3: "PARAMETERS"]

Number three. Parameters.

The knobs. The billions of little numbers inside the model that got adjusted during training. "A seventy-billion-parameter model" means seventy billion knobs.

More knobs usually means more capable, but also slower and more expensive. Not always better, though. How it was trained matters a lot too.

[SLIDE 5: Term 4: "TRAINING vs INFERENCE"]

Number four. Training versus inference. Two for one, because they go together.

Training is when the AI learns. Months, huge cost, happens once. Inference is when you use it. Every time you hit send, that's inference.

If training is going to cooking school, inference is cooking dinner every night.

[SLIDE 6: Term 5: "FINE-TUNING"]

Number five. Fine-tuning.

Taking a model that's already trained and giving it extra training on something specific. Legal documents. Customer support chats. A company's writing style.

It's like a general doctor doing extra training to become a specialist. Same person, sharper at one thing.

[SLIDE 7: Term 6: "CONTEXT WINDOW" with the whiteboard]

Number six. Context window.

The whiteboard. How much text the AI can look at in one go. Your chat, any documents you pasted, all of it has to fit.

When companies brag about a "bigger context window," they mean a bigger whiteboard. More room before it starts forgetting.

[SLIDE 8: Term 7: "MULTIMODAL" with text, image, audio, video icons]

Number seven. Multimodal.

AI that handles more than just text. You can show it a photo, talk to it, sometimes give it video. "Modes" just means types of input.

So snapping a picture of a weird rash, or a broken washing machine... wait, maybe show a doctor the rash. But the washing machine, sure. That's multimodal.

[SLIDE 9: Term 8: "GPU" with a chip]

Number eight. GPU.

The chips AI runs on. Originally made for video games, because they're great at doing tons of simple math at the same time. Turns out that's exactly what AI needs.

When you hear about companies spending billions on AI, a huge chunk of that is GPUs. It's why the company Nvidia became so enormous.

[SLIDE 10: Term 9: "API" with two apps connected by a cable]

Number nine. API.

As a software engineer, this is my world. An API is how one piece of software talks to another. Like a drive-through window. You don't go into the kitchen. You order through the window, and the food comes out.

When an app says it's "powered by GPT" or "built on Claude," it's usually ordering through that company's API window.

[SLIDE 11: Term 10: "BENCHMARK" with a report card]

Number ten. Benchmark.

A standard test for AI. A set of questions, the same for every model, so you can compare scores. Math tests. Coding tests. Exam questions.

Here's why it matters. Every new AI launch comes with charts saying "we beat everyone on benchmarks." But models can end up tuned to do well on the specific test, the way a student can cram for one exam. High scores are a good sign. They're not the whole story. The real test is whether it's good at your stuff.

[SLIDE 12: All ten terms on one slide, timer stopped]

And... time. Ten terms. LLM, token, parameters, training and inference, fine-tuning, context window, multimodal, GPU, API, benchmark.
